        Method & caveats

        The district, year and subject filters don't change this page.

        Data sources and years

        • Spending: SACS. California Department of Education (CDE) Standardized Account Code Structure, unaudited actuals, for fiscal years 2022-23, 2023-24 and 2024-25. The page uses General Fund (Fund 01) operating expenditures: objects 1000–5999 and 7300–7399. "Function" (instruction, pupil services, administration and so on) and "kind of dollar" (the funding source's restriction, from the resource code) both come from the SACS accounts.
        • Test results: CAASPP. Smarter Balanced ELA and math, percent of students meeting or exceeding the standard, all students and all tested grades, for spring 2023, 2024 and 2025 (school years 2022-23 to 2024-25).
        • How they are joined. Both sources are matched on the 14-digit County-District-School (CDS) code, the CDE standard identifier, using a maintained lookup from CAASPP's identifiers to CDS codes. A year missing from either source is kept and flagged, never dropped.
        • Per student means spending ÷ average daily attendance (ADA) from SACS. For Los Angeles Unified and Inglewood Unified, ADA includes charter schools reported in the district's General Fund, because their spending is in it too.
        • Coverage. All 79 Los Angeles County school districts, as counted by CDE and SACS. County offices of education and independently reporting charter schools are not included.
        • Checks. Four districts were checked line by line against their own board-approved financial filings and the CAASPP website: Los Angeles Unified, Redondo Beach Unified, Hughes-Elizabeth Lakes Union Elementary and Whittier Union High. They match to the cent, except one documented 2023-24 line for Los Angeles Unified. The other 75 come from the same pipeline but were not individually hand-checked.

        What this tool does not claim

        • It is not a fraud or wrongdoing tool. Nothing on this page suggests any district misused money.
        • It does not rank districts. There is no return-on-investment score, composite index or league table, and the district list is alphabetical.
        • It does not find that spending "doesn't work." Showing spending and scores side by side is a starting point for questions, not an answer to them.

        The funding-formula confound

        California's Local Control Funding Formula (LCFF) gives every district a base grant per student, plus supplemental and concentration grants for each student who is low-income, an English learner or in foster care. Districts with more high-need students therefore receive, and spend, more per student.

        Students facing those challenges also tend to score lower on state tests, for reasons that have little to do with how the district spends its money. So higher spending can go with lower scores without implying waste. The extra money is there because the need is there. A district that spends more and scores lower may be doing well given who it serves, and this page cannot tell you either way.

        Suppressed and missing values

        CAASPP withholds a score when too few students tested, to protect privacy. On this page, any withheld or missing value is shown as "not reported", never as zero and never as a blank chart. A zero would wrongly say no student met the standard. For 2022-23 to 2024-25, no district-level score in Los Angeles County was withheld; the handling is in place for future data.

        Known limits

        • District level only. SACS reports spending by district, so this page cannot say how money is spent at any one school.
        • Test scores are one contested outcome measure. They leave out much of what schools do, such as attendance, graduation, the arts, wellbeing and more.
        • Correlation is not causation. Spending and scores moving together, or apart, does not show that one caused the other.
        • Don't compare across district types. Elementary districts test only grades within 3–8, high school districts (9-12) test grade 11, and unified districts test both. They also have different cost structures.
        • Three years. 2022-23 to 2024-25 includes the end of one-time federal pandemic relief (ESSER), which moved spending in many districts.
